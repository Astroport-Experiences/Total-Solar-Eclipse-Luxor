What is a total solar eclipse?

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun. In a total eclipse, the Moon covers the Sun's bright disc completely. For a short time you can see the Sun's faint outer atmosphere, the corona, with your eyes.

It works because of a coincidence: the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and also about 400 times farther away. From Earth, both look almost exactly the same size.

The Moon's darkest shadow, the umbra, is only about 100–250 km wide, and it races across Earth at over 1,700 km/h. Only people inside this narrow path of totality see the total eclipse. Everyone else sees a partial one.

Total

The Moon covers the Sun completely and the corona appears.

Annular

The Moon is farther away and looks too small, leaving a "ring of fire".

Partial

The Moon covers only part of the Sun, like a bite taken out of it.

Hybrid

Rare: total from some places on Earth and annular from others.

The phases of a total eclipse

  1. First contact

    The Moon's edge first touches the Sun and the partial eclipse begins. This phase lasts about an hour or more.

  2. Partial phase

    The light fades and turns strange and sharp. The air cools. Shadows under trees become small crescents.

  3. Baily's beads & diamond ring

    The last sunlight shines through valleys on the Moon's edge, making glittering beads and one brilliant "diamond".

  4. Totality

    Darkness falls. The pearly corona, the pink prominences, and bright planets and stars appear. A 360° sunset glows on the horizon. This is the only time it is safe to look without eclipse glasses.

  5. Third contact

    The diamond ring appears on the other side. Put your glasses back on.

  6. Fourth contact

    The Moon leaves the Sun's disc completely and the eclipse is over.

Watch safely

✔ Do

  • Use certified eclipse glasses that meet the ISO 12312-2 standard, or a handheld solar viewer.
  • Check your glasses for scratches or holes before you use them.
  • Put solar filters on the front of telescopes, binoculars and camera lenses.
  • Try a pinhole projector, or look at the crescents in the shadows of leaves.
  • Take your glasses off only during totality, then put them back on as soon as the light returns.

✘ Don't

  • Look at the partly covered Sun with your bare eyes, even when only a little is showing.
  • Use sunglasses (even very dark ones), smoked glass, X-ray film or CDs.
  • Look through a telescope, binoculars or a camera viewfinder without a proper solar filter, even while wearing eclipse glasses.
  • Leave children alone while they watch.

Damage to the eye (solar retinopathy) can happen without any pain, and it can be permanent.

Frequently asked questions

How often do total solar eclipses happen?

About once every 18 months somewhere on Earth. But any single spot sees one only about once every 375 years on average.

Why doesn't an eclipse happen every new moon?

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° compared with Earth's orbit. At most new moons, the Moon's shadow passes above or below Earth.

How long does totality last?

From a few seconds to a maximum of about 7½ minutes. It depends on how far away the Moon is, where Earth is in its orbit, and where you stand inside the path.

Is a 99% partial eclipse nearly as good?

No. Even 1% of the Sun is still dazzlingly bright. The corona, the darkness and the stars appear only at 100%, so it's worth travelling into the path of totality.

Can I photograph it with my phone?

Yes. During the partial phases, hold a solar filter over the lens. During totality, remove the filter and lock the exposure. Don't forget to stop and simply look, too.
